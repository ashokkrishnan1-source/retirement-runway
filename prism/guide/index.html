How to use Prism

Prism reads one screener download and gives you a short reading list: the best businesses in the file, and which of them are on sale. This guide shows how to read it, one short lesson at a time.

Open Prism

A Show me in Prism button opens Prism at the right place. ← Back to the guide brings you back to the lesson.

Prism lists companies to research. It does not tell you what to buy.

Start here about ten minutes

1Look around with the made-up companiesNo file needed

Prism opens with made-up companies, so you can see how it reads before you load anything. Their symbols end in .X. If you have already loaded a file, you will see your own companies, and this lesson works the same way.

  1. The card at the top, Today's file, says what is loaded.
  2. Under it is the list. Its title is the answer: how many A-class businesses are on sale. The sentence below the title says what put them there.
  3. Each row is one company. It says in a few words what Prism found, then what the price is counting on and the most to pay.
  4. Tap any ?, or any small tag in a row, for a note about it. In an opened company, tap any underlined name.
  5. Tap a row to open the figures behind it. Tap it again to close it.

The page at a glance. Prism has more on it than you need on day one. This is the order things come in.

2Load your own fileOne download, every column

Prism works on one file: the whole-market screener download from your data provider, with one row for every stock.

  1. At your data provider, download the screener with every column it offers. A CSV file loads fastest. Excel also works.
  2. Press Load a screener file, or drop the file anywhere on the page.
  3. Read the line under Today's file. It gives the file's name, the number of companies and the date of the prices.
  4. Open Columns found in the file once. It lists the columns Prism found, and the ones it looked for and did not find. The more it finds, the fewer answers come out as "unknown".

Good to know. The file is read in this browser and is never uploaded. Prism keeps it in this browser, so the page reopens where you left off. Forget the stored file, under Settings, removes it.

Good to know. Show example brings the made-up companies back. Your own file returns when you reload the page. If a file lacks the columns the A-list needs, Prism opens on the five-question list instead.

3Read a rowWhat each part of a row tells you

A row says in words what Prism found, so you can decide in a few seconds whether the company is worth opening.

  • What Prism foundA few words on why the company is on this list. On the on-sale list it gives the P/E against the company's usual P/E, the growth the price is counting on against the growth the company has delivered, and how far the price is off its high.
  • The small tagsHolding up, One weak spot or Slipping says whether the business itself shows signs of weakening. Rising or Falling says which way the price is moving. Cyclical marks an industry whose profits swing. Tap a tag for what it means for that company.
  • Price impliesThe yearly growth today's price needs to be fair, summed up in a word: Decline, No growth, Moderate growth, High growth or Perfection. The lower it is, the less the price is counting on.
  • Entry ceilingThe most to pay. The line under it gives today's price and how far that is under or over the ceiling. A later lesson explains it.
  • VerdictYour own decision about the company. Prism never sets one.

Tap the row to open it. The detail shows the six tests of the business, the signs of a low price, the signs of slipping, the dividend, the signs that the business is winning, two boxes on its management, the five questions and a place for your verdict.

Then, the question you have

Which businesses are worth owning?The whole A-list

The A-list grades the business alone and ignores the price. A company gets on it by passing six tests.

  • It earns a high return on the money invested in it, over five years and now.
  • It has made a profit every year for a long run of years.
  • Its sales keep growing.
  • Its profits arrive as cash.
  • Its share count is steady.
  • It carries little debt.
  1. Under Show, choose The whole A-list.
  2. Open a row. Is it an A-class business? lists the six tests with the company's own figures, and a tick or a cross for each.
  3. Lower down, one line gives the dividend: what it pays a year, how many years it has been paid and raised, and how much of the company's spare cash it takes.
  4. Disagree with a grade? Under My A-list, choose Always on my A-list or Never on my A-list. Your choice wins.

Good to know. A bank or an insurer has six tests of its own, because debt is its raw material. Its row carries the tag Bank or insurer: tap it for the tests.

Good to know. A test the file can't answer does not count as a pass. To include companies that miss exactly one test, open More options and tick Put companies that miss just one of the six tests on the A-list too.

Good to know. Each test's pass mark is shown beside it. Tap a test's name for a note on it. The pass marks are starting points. None has been tested against later returns.

Want each test explained? See "What does each of the six tests ask?", further down this guide.

Which of them are on sale?A-list on sale, the list Prism opens on

A good business is worth reading about when its price is low. Prism calls an A-list company "on sale" when at least two of three signs are present.

  • Its P/E is well below its own average of past years.
  • The price is counting on much less growth than its sales have delivered.
  • The price is well below its high of the past year.
  1. Under Show, choose A-list on sale. Prism opens here.
  2. Read the first line of each row. It names the signs that are present.
  3. Look at the tag. Holding up means the business shows none of four signs of weakening: slowing sales, falling returns, a weak forecast, falling profit. Slipping means it shows two or more. Businesses that are holding up come first.
  4. Open a row. Is the price low? and Is the business slipping? show each sign with the company's own figures. Tap a sign's name for its rule.

Good to know. A low price with a slipping business is often a trap. Read why it is slipping before anything else.

One worry, many names. When one sector fills a large part of the list, a line under the opening sentence says so. Several companies marked down by the same worry are one bet, not several.

What is the most I should pay?Entry ceiling · Price implies

The entry ceiling is the most to pay for a share. Prism works it out from the company's spare cash: what is left each year after running the business and investing in it.

  • Pay the ceiling and you would still earn a good return each year, even if that cash grows only slowly. Pay less and you earn more.
  • "Under" means today's price is below the ceiling. "Over" means the price is counting on faster growth. A price far above the ceiling is shown as a multiple of it.
  • Some rows say "None" and give the reason: a cyclical industry, no spare cash, a data warning, or a price that already assumes very fast growth.
  • Price implies looks at the same thing from the other side: the growth today's price needs.
  1. Tap the ceiling in any row. A note explains it in plain words, with that company's own figures.
  2. To put first the companies whose price is furthest below their ceiling, set Order to Most under the entry ceiling. Companies with no ceiling go last.

Good to know. It is a "don't pay more than this" line. It is not what the share is worth, and it is not a forecast. The ceiling is cautious on purpose, so a price above it is not a reason to sell.

Which could I sell puts on?Put desk

The put desk is for readers who get into a company by selling cash-secured puts. It lists A-list companies that are on sale, or fell hard in the past week, and that have listed options.

  1. Under Show, choose Put desk.
  2. Read the row: how far the price fell this week, how far it moves in a typical day, and where the price stands against the entry ceiling.
  3. Check the earnings tag. It gives the days to the next report and turns amber when the report is close. An option that expires after that date carries whatever the report brings.
  4. Look for an ex-dividend tag. It appears when the company has declared its next dividend and the date is near. It matters most if you are assigned and then sell a call: a call that is in the money is often exercised just before that date, and the shares go with the dividend.
  5. Read the option prices at your broker. They are not in the file.

Good to know. Prism never suggests a strike. It has no option prices, and a guess would mislead.

Good to know. The lesson "Record what you decided" shows how to note a put you have sold.

What is cheap and out of favor?The five questions · Near misses

This is a second way of looking, separate from the A-list. Prism asks every company five questions and keeps those that answer yes to all five. Most of the answers compare a company with others in its own sector.

  • Good business?It earns high returns on the money invested in it, compared with its sector.
  • Low expectations?The price is low against cash flow and earnings, so little growth is needed to justify it.
  • Strong enough to wait?It has little debt and is not issuing many new shares.
  • Growing per share?Sales and cash flow per share are rising, or the share count is shrinking.
  • Out of favor?The share price has lagged its sector over the past year.
  1. Under Show, choose Yes to all five questions.
  2. For more names, choose Near misses: one question short. Each row names the question it misses.
  3. To stop requiring a question, open More options and press it under The five questions.
  4. Open a row to see each answer with the figures behind it. Tap a figure's name for what it is.

Five answers, never one score. A single score would let a very cheap price make up for a weak business. Here a company must pass each question on its own.

Good to know. An answer the file can't give is "unknown", and unknown is never a pass.

What are the nine named lists?Particular situations worth a look

Each named list is a situation that is often worth reading about. It shows every company in that situation, and each row says how the company answered the five questions. Above the rows are two prompts: First check and It is wrong if.

  • Quality on saleA strong business at a low price, both judged against its own sector.
  • Priced for decline, still growingThe price only makes sense if cash flow shrinks for years, yet sales are growing.
  • Growth without a reratingSales grew strongly while the share price went nowhere.
  • Cash-backedNet cash is a large part of the market value, and the business still earns cash.
  • Shrinking share countThe company retired a meaningful part of its stock, paid for out of its own cash.
  • Fallen qualityDown sharply in a year, but still a good business with a sound balance sheet.
  • Industry leader at a discountOne of the best businesses in its industry, priced below most of its peers.
  • Cash improving, returns still lowA weak business whose cash flow is rising fast. A possible early repair, not proof of one.
  • Cycle survivorsA cyclical company that has fallen hard but has little debt and still earns cash.

Tap a name to open that list in Prism.

What about long shots?Moonshots

Moonshots are small companies growing fast that can pay their own way for now. Most companies like these disappoint. Prism can check that a company is able to survive. It cannot check that its story is true.

  1. Under Show, choose Moonshots.
  2. Read the row: sales growth, gross margin, how many years the cash lasts at today's spending, and the change in the share count.
  3. Treat the list as a place to start reading, never as a list to buy.

Good to know. Cyclical industries are left out. When several moonshots come from one sector, Prism says so, because they are one bet.

Is the management any good?How well is it run · How does it treat its owners

Two old tests of a management: how well do they run the business, and how well do they treat its owners. Prism puts figures to both, from the file you loaded.

  1. Open a company's row. Under the signs are two boxes, How well is it run? and How does it treat its owners?. Each heading gives the gist. Tap one to open it.
  2. How well is it run? sets the company beside the Typical company of its industry in your file, and beside its nearest rivals. Under each measure is Ahead, Middle or Behind, and how many of the others it beats.
  3. To choose the rivals yourself, type their symbols in the field that says Compare with: type symbols.
  4. Where a year's cash went takes every hundred dollars the business brought in and says how much went back into the business, on acquisitions, to the owners and to lenders.
  5. How does it treat its owners? says how much of the company its directors and officers own, whether the share count rose or fell, what came back as dividends and buybacks, and what was paid to staff in new shares.
  • The typical company is the hand the whole industry was dealt. A company far ahead of it in a hard industry has done more than one a little ahead in an easy one.
  • Prism picks the rivals nearest in size. The industry labels are the data provider's, and they can be rough: choose your own rivals when they are.
  • A bank or an insurer is set beside other banks and insurers, on measures that fit them.

Good to know. One file gives one year and some five-year averages. These are facts to weigh, not a score, and they play no part in any list.

Good to know. How the managers pay themselves is in the company's proxy statement. No download carries it. The next lesson shows how to have it read.

What do its filings say?Deeper dive · Research from the filings · Where does it stand?

Some answers are in no download: who a company's rivals are and what they say of it, what its managers did with its cash over ten years, how they pay themselves. They are in the annual report and the proxy statement that a company files with the regulator.

Reading those takes a reader, and Prism has no AI. So the reading is done outside this page, by Claude, and Prism shows what comes back. Prism works the same without it.

  1. Open a company you want to know more about. At the top, press Deeper dive. The button turns into In the queue. Do the same for each company you want read.
  2. Go down to Research from the filings. Your queue is listed there. The × beside a symbol takes it out again.
  3. On your claude.ai link, the queue is kept online, in a part only you can read. In a Claude Code session for these tools, say "read my queue". The research arrives on this page by itself, and the company leaves the queue.
  4. Anywhere else, press Download the queue and give the file to Claude in such a session. A research file comes back: press Load a research file and choose it.
  5. Open a company that was read. A new box, Where does it stand?, says what the company does, then gives Clear warnings, Clear strengths, Who it names as rivals, what other companies' reports say of it, and What worries it.
  6. How well is it run? now sets the company beside the rivals it names itself, and adds Ten years of cash, from its filings and From its filings.
  7. How does it treat its owners? adds From its proxy statement: what the chief executive was paid, and what the pay depends on.
  • Under every finding is the passage it rests on, copied from the filing, with a link to the filing. A program checked each passage. A finding whose passage was not in the filing was thrown away.
  • A reading takes about a quarter of an hour a company and uses your Claude plan. Queue the few companies you are close to acting on, not a whole list.
  • A newer annual report is out means the company has filed one since the research was done. Open the company and press Read again.
  • The research stays in this browser, like your screener file. On your claude.ai link it is also kept in the part only you can read. Forget the research removes it from both.

Good to know. A company writes these reports with its lawyers. They say little that is frank about a rival, and nothing about what happens next. Most managements sit in a murky middle that is hard to rank: the research shows the evidence, and leaves the judgment to you.

It is an AI's reading. The passages are checked, the summaries are not. Read the passage, and follow the link when it matters. It is research, not investment advice.

A company's page, line by line the detail

What does each of the six tests ask?Is it an A-class business?

Open any company. Its first box, Is it an A-class business?, has a line for each test: the company's own figure, a tick or a cross, and what the test needs.

Tap the underlined name at the start of a line. A note opens, written for that company: what the test asks, its figures, the pass mark and why it matters.

  • Return on capitalFor every hundred dollars put into the business, by its owners and its lenders together, how much profit does it make in a year? The line gives the average of five years and the latest year, and each has its own mark. A business that earns a lot on each hundred dollars can grow without asking anyone for more money.
  • ProfitableHow many years in a row it has made a profit. A long run takes in a hard stretch for most industries. A business that stayed in profit through one can take a knock.
  • Sales growthHow fast sales grew each year, over five years and over ten. The mark is low on purpose: it asks only that the business is not standing still.
  • Cash from operationsProfit is an accountant's figure. Cash from operations is the money that actually came in. The test asks whether the profit turned up as cash. When cash stays well below profit, find out why.
  • Share countHow much the number of shares changed in a year. Every new share makes your slice a little smaller. A falling count means the company is buying its own shares back.
  • More cash than debt, or Debt after its cashCan it comfortably carry what it owes? A company with more cash than debt passes outright. One that owes more than it holds is asked two things. Is its debt small against what the business earns in a year? And does its profit cover the interest several times over? The line gives both figures and both marks.

What is EBITDA? The debt line sets debt against EBITDA: what the business earns in a year before interest, tax and the wearing out of its equipment. Think of it as the yearly earnings there are to pay lenders with.

A bank or an insurer has six tests of its own: Return on its owners' money, Profitable, Sales growth, Return on everything it holds, Share count and Its owners' money as a share of what it holds. Debt is its raw material, so it is not tested on debt.

Reading the marks. A tick passes and a cross misses. A question mark means the file cannot say, which does not count as a pass. The pass marks are starting points that you can change under Settings. None has been tested against later returns.

Low price, or weak business? Line by lineIs the price low? · Is the business slipping?

Under the six tests are two questions that Prism asks of every A-list company. Each has its own lines. A line ends in a word, not a tick, so there is nothing to work out: "sign" or "no sign" for the price, "fine" or "weak" for the business.

Is the price low? Two of the three signs are needed.

  • Against its own pastThe P/E is what you pay for each dollar of yearly profit. The line sets today's P/E beside the company's own average over the years. A company is compared with itself, because some businesses always carry a high P/E.
  • Against its growthPrism works out the growth a year that today's price needs to be fair, and sets it beside the growth the company has delivered. The sign is there when the price expects much less than the record.
  • Against its highHow far the price is below its highest of the past year. A fall says the mood has changed. It does not say that the business has.

Is the business slipping? Two weak spots make it Slipping. One is only a tag, One weak spot. None is Holding up.

  • Latest quarterSales in the latest quarter against the same quarter a year before, set beside the company's own usual pace.
  • ReturnsWhat it earns now on the money put into it, against its own five-year average. Falling returns often show before falling profit.
  • ForecastThe sales growth that analysts expect next year, against the usual pace. Forecasts are often wrong, so this is one sign among four and no more.
  • ProfitWhether operating profit rose or fell over the year. Operating profit is what is left of sales after the costs of running the business.

Good to know. Each mark has an exact rule: how far below its average, how far under its pace. Tap the name at the start of a line. The note gives the rule and the company's own figures.

Why two. One quarter can be noise: a late order, or a strong quarter a year ago. So one weak spot is a reason to look, and two are a reason to find out what is wrong before anything else.

What are the figures behind the five questions?Sector rank, and each measure in plain words

Lower in an opened company are five boxes, one for each question. A box gives the answer, a sentence that says why, and the measures behind it. Beside each measure is the company's figure and its sector rank.

The rank says where the company stands on that measure among the companies of its own sector, on a scale where the top is the best. The answer to a question comes from the average of its ranks. Prism ranks inside a sector because a margin that is good for a supermarket is poor for a software company.

Tap any measure's name for a note with the company's own figure and rank.

  • Good business?Return on capital and Return on capital, 5-year: the profit on each hundred dollars put into the business, now and over five years.
    Gross margin: of each hundred dollars of sales, what is left after paying for what was sold.
    Operating margin: what is left after all the costs of running the business.
    Free cash flow margin: how much of each hundred dollars of sales ends up as spare cash.
  • Low expectations?Each measure sets something the business makes against what it costs to buy, so higher means a lower price.
    Earnings yield: a year's profit as a share of the price, which is the P/E turned upside down.
    Forward earnings yield: the same, on the profit analysts expect next year.
    Free cash flow ÷ enterprise value: a year's spare cash against what it would cost to buy the whole company and pay off its debts.
    EBITDA ÷ enterprise value: the yearly earnings before interest, tax and wear, against that same cost.
    Cash flow after stock pay ÷ market value: spare cash, less what staff were paid in new shares, against what the company is worth on the market.
  • Strong enough to wait?A plain test, with no ranks.
    Net cash: cash less debt.
    Debt ÷ EBITDA: debt against a year's earnings before interest, tax and wear.
    Share count change: new shares issued in a year.
    Interest cover: how many times the profit covers the interest.
  • Growing per share?Sales growth and Sales growth, 3-year: how fast sales grew, over one year and over three.
    Free cash flow per share growth and Earnings per share growth: growth in the spare cash and in the profit for each share.
    Share count change and Buyback yield: fewer shares mean that each one owns more.
  • Out of favor?Here a weaker share price ranks higher.
    One-year return: what an owner made or lost in a year, dividends included.
    Below 52-week high: how far the price is below its highest of the past year.

Good to know. Each measure in How well is it run? opens a note in the same way.

Five answers, never one score. The ranks feed one question each. Prism never adds the questions up.

Keeping track

Record what you decidedVerdicts, and how to note a put

A verdict is your own decision about a company. Prism remembers it from file to file, so you don't read the same company again tomorrow.

  • WatchInteresting, and you are waiting for something.
  • ActAn order or a put is open.
  • OwnYou hold the shares.
  • No actionA fine company at the wrong price.
  • RejectHide it from every list.
  1. For a quick verdict, use the menu in the Verdict column of any row.
  2. For the full record, open the row. Under Your verdict, fill in Thesis, in one line, Why it is cheap, Trigger: what would change this verdict and a Review by date. Press Save verdict.
  3. If you sell a put to get in, choose Act, write the strike and expiry under Entry: what you did, or plan to do, and set Review by to the expiry date. If the shares are assigned to you, change the verdict to Own, and note any call you sell in the same way.
  4. Your verdicts, below the list, shows the companies you follow, with the review date first. A date that has passed is marked Due. The Signs column counts how each business is doing. The next lesson explains it.

Good to know. To see rejected companies again, open More options and tick Show companies I rejected. Change the verdict to bring one back.

Good to know. Download case notes saves your verdicts as a file. Load case notes reads one back.

Is the business itself winning?A few signs, read again after each report

A low price is one thing. Whether the business is getting better is another. Prism reads a few figures after each report a company makes, and says how each one stands.

  1. Open a company's row and find Is the business winning?. For a company you follow, with a verdict of Watch, Act or Own, it is already open. For any other, tap its heading.
  2. Read it as says what kind of business Prism takes it for. The signs depend on the kind: a consultancy is read on Sales per employee, a chip maker on Inventory turns a year. If Prism has the kind wrong, choose another.
  3. Each line is one sign. Now is the latest figure. Compared with is what it is set against, most often the figure a year before.
  4. Reading is one word: Better, Worse or The same. A small change counts as the same. Too early means there is nothing to set the figure against yet.
  5. Since the report before says which way the sign moved last, once Prism has two reports on record.
  6. Tap a sign's name for what it measures, and why this company got that reading.

Signs of your own. Some of the best signs are in no download. They are in the company's own report: new bookings for a consultancy, sales at stores open a year for a chain of shops.

  1. Under Your own signs, tap one that Prism suggests. Or type a name in A sign of your own, choose Higher is better, Lower is better or I will judge it myself, and press Add.
  2. After each report, type the figure beside it, and the figure a year before, which the same report gives. Prism keeps both and gives a reading.
  3. A sign with nothing typed for the latest report is marked To fill in.
  • Prism keeps the figures of each report for the companies you follow and for the A-list. Report by report, at the foot of the box, shows them side by side. You can fill in or correct your own figures for an earlier report there.
  • When a company you follow has reported, the line that begins Since your last file says so, and Your verdicts marks it New report.
  • On the first day, the figure a year before is worked out from the growth figures in your file. After that Prism uses its own record.

Good to know. Prism does not add the signs up into a score, and it does not forecast. Whether the company is winning is your call. The signs play no part in any list.

Good to know. If the box says Load your file again, the copy of your file in this browser is from before Prism read these figures. Load the file once more and they appear.

What changed since my last file?The line above the list

Once you have loaded a second, newer file, Prism compares the two.

  • A line appears between the file card and the list. It begins Since your last file. Open it to see who joined and who left each main list, with the reason.
  • For the companies you follow, with a verdict of Watch, Act or Own, it flags what needs attention: a review that is due, earnings coming up, new results and how the signs stand after them, a sharp fall this week, a company that left the A-list or began to slip, and a price that crossed its entry ceiling.
  • In the list itself, a company that was not there in your previous file carries the tag New.

Good to know. The line is hidden when there is nothing to say, and it never appears with the made-up companies.

Have the lists been any good?Scorecard

Nothing in Prism has been tested against the past. The scorecard is how the lists earn or lose your trust.

  1. Keep loading newer files. Each time, Prism notes which companies have joined each list, and at what price.
  2. Open Scorecard: how the lists have done. For each list it shows how the middle company has done since joining, next to the typical large company over the same days.
  3. Give it time. Prism says so plainly when the history is under three months.

Good to know. The figures are on price alone, so dividends are left out. A company that left the file or split its shares is dropped. A list that looks good over a few months has proved nothing.

Good to know. The scorecard starts when you load your own file.

Keeping it

Change what Prism looks forMore options · Settings · How this works
  • More options, above the rows, holds Company size, Find in this list, the boxes for data warnings and rejected companies, and The five questions. Its heading tells you when a setting is not the usual one.
  • Settings, near the bottom, holds the pass marks. They are starting points, not tested rules. Change one and every list updates. Back to the starting values undoes your changes.
  • Leave out mining and coal companies and Symbols to leave out remove companies you never want to see.
  • How this works, and its limits explains every test in full, and what the figures can't show. Read it once.

Good to know. Hide companies with data warnings is ticked to start with. A figure that looks too good is usually a one-off or an error, so those rows stay hidden until you untick it.

Words explained

The terms Prism usesA-list, on sale, entry ceiling and the rest
A-class business, A-list
A business that passes Prism's six tests of the business itself, with the price left out. The A-list is the set of them, adjusted by your own choices.
On sale
An A-list company whose price is low on at least two of three counts: against its own past, against its growth record and against its high.
Holding up, slipping
Whether the business shows signs of weakening: slowing sales, falling returns, a weak forecast, falling profit.
Spare cash
What a company has left each year after running the business and investing in it. Analysts call it free cash flow.
Entry ceiling
The most to pay: the price at which you would still earn a good return each year if the spare cash grows only slowly.
Price implies
The yearly growth today's price needs to be fair.
Return on capital
What a business earns each year on the money invested in it.
P/E
The share price divided by a year's profit per share. A low P/E means the price expects little.
Sector rank
Where a company stands among the companies of its own sector, from 0 to 100.
Margin
What is left of each dollar of sales. Gross margin is what is left after paying for what was sold. Operating margin is what is left after all the costs of running the business.
EBITDA
What a business earns in a year before interest, tax and the wearing out of its equipment. Lenders look at it, because it is what there is to pay them with.
Market value
What the whole company is worth at today's share price: the price times the number of shares.
Enterprise value
What it would cost to buy the whole company and pay off its debts: the market value, plus the debt, less the cash.
Earnings yield
A year's profit as a share of the price. It is the P/E turned upside down, so a higher yield means a lower price.
Net cash
A company's cash less its debt. Above zero, it could pay off all it owes and have cash left.
Interest cover
How many times a company's operating profit covers the yearly interest on its debt.
Share count
The number of shares a company has. New shares make each owner's slice smaller. Buybacks make it larger.
Cyclical
An industry whose profits swing with the economy or a commodity price, so one good year can flatter it.
Data warning
A figure that looks too good to be true. It is usually a one-off or an error.
Ex-dividend date
The date from which a buyer of the share no longer gets the next dividend. Whoever owns the share the day before does. Prism shows the date once the company has declared it, and does not guess before then.
Cash-secured put
A promise to buy shares at a set price, the strike, up to a set date, the expiry, in return for a payment now. You keep the cash to pay for them set aside. If the price ends below the strike, the shares are assigned to you.
Verdict
Your own decision about a company, kept from file to file.
Annual report
The account of its year that a company must file with the regulator. In it the company says what it does, who it competes with and what worries it.
Proxy statement
What a company sends its owners before the yearly meeting. It says what the managers were paid, what the pay depends on, and how much of the company they own.
Sign
A figure that shows whether a business is getting better, read again after each report. Which signs fit depends on the kind of business.
Report
The results a company publishes, most often every three months. Prism files a company's figures under the date its latest reported period ended.

This guide explains how to use the tool. Prism lists companies to research. It does not tell you what to buy, and none of its tests has been checked against later returns. It is general information for your own research, not investment advice.
