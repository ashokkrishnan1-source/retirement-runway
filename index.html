Should your own 401(k) contributions go in pre-tax or as Roth? Pre-tax 401(k) saving lowers tax now but grows the IRA that required withdrawals come from later. Roth saving costs tax now and is tax-free later. This compares your choices while you work. Pay covers living costs either way: extra tax on Roth saving comes from cash or brokerage, and pay you don't contribute is saved in brokerage. Employer match always goes in pre-tax.

How this works

The three plans. Typical spends cash above your floor, then pays the bills from the IRA. Brokerage first spends cash above your floor, then brokerage, then IRA, then Roth. Brokerage first + conversions can also convert while you're both still working, up to a bracket or a fixed amount, with the extra tax paid from cash above your floor, then brokerage. Once either of you retires, it adds a Roth conversion every year sized to your chosen limit, through the age you pick. While a spouse still works, their wages already fill the low brackets, so only the 22% or 24% limits convert much in those years. Conversion tax is paid from cash above your floor, then from brokerage (including tax on any gains from selling shares). If those can't cover it, the tool converts less: it never uses IRA or Roth money, or your cash floor, to pay conversion tax.
Brokerage gains. Only the gain part of a sale is taxed. Your cost basis is tracked year by year, so the gain share rises as the account grows.
Social Security. Enter the monthly benefit at full retirement age from ssa.gov/myaccount, in today's dollars. Claiming early cuts it (about 30% at 62); waiting adds 8% a year to 70. A spouse gets the larger of their own or half the other's, once the other has claimed. After a death, the survivor gets the larger of their own benefit or a survivor benefit. The survivor benefit is the deceased's actual check if they had claimed (at least 82.5% of their full-age amount); 100% of their full-age amount if they died before full retirement age without claiming; or that plus the delayed credits earned up to death if they died later without claiming. A survivor can start survivor benefits from 60 at 71.5%, rising to 100% at their own full retirement age. If you claim before full retirement age while still working, the earnings test withholds $1 of benefits for every $2 of pay above the yearly limit, including benefits a spouse receives on that worker's record, and those months are credited back at full retirement age. From 63 you can set a lower conversion target ("From age 63, convert up to"), applied once anyone is within two years of Medicare. 401(k) contributions are capped at the IRS limit plus the catch-up for your age; you can split them between pre-tax and Roth, and catch-up contributions are Roth when last year's pay was over the IRS limit. When Roth saving or smaller contributions change your take-home pay, the difference is saved in (or paid from) cash and brokerage, since pay covers living costs. Benefits are paid in full as scheduled unless you tick "Assume a Social Security cut in my plan" (or test one in the what-if). A cut is modeled as a flat percentage off every benefit, own, spousal and survivor, from the year you choose, for life; a real fix could instead protect current retirees, phase in, or raise taxes rather than cut benefits.
Pensions. Enter the yearly amount your plan statement shows when payments begin, the starting age, any yearly increase (many private pensions have none, so they lose buying power over time), and the share your spouse keeps after your death (often 0%, 50% or 100%, depending on the payout option you choose). Pensions are taxed as ordinary income and fill the low brackets before IRA withdrawals or conversions.
RMDs. Start at 73 or 75, depending on your birth year, using the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table. Extra RMD money is reinvested in brokerage.
State taxes. Pick your state now and in retirement. The tool uses each state's 2026 rules in simplified form (reviewed November 2026): its brackets in the range most retirees fall in, whether and above what income it taxes Social Security, and its main exclusion for IRA and 401(k) income, including income limits and whether Roth conversions qualify (for example, Pennsylvania and Illinois exempt retirement income entirely). City and county taxes are skipped, except average county tax in Indiana and Maryland. Typing your own rate replaces the brackets with that flat rate.
Views. Plan shows the long-term projection and what-ifs. This year turns the plan into a monthly paycheck, tax payments and a to-do list. Health shows marketplace premiums and subsidies before 65 and Medicare surcharges from 65. Legacy shows what heirs receive by account type.
Health costs. If you choose a marketplace plan, the tool charges the benchmark premium for each retired person under 65 and subtracts the premium subsidy: you pay a share of income (set each year by the IRS), with no subsidy above 400% of the poverty line. From 65, Medicare surcharges (IRMAA) are added when income two years earlier passed the limits. Both count as costs, so conversions and withdrawals are judged with them included, and you can keep conversions under either limit.
Giving. Charitable giving is part of your spending. From 70½ it is paid straight from the IRA as a qualified charitable distribution, which counts toward required withdrawals and isn't taxed.
IRA withdrawals before 59½. Withdrawals before the year you turn 60 pay a 10% penalty, so each plan uses cash, brokerage and Roth first and touches a penalized IRA last. Roth withdrawals are assumed to come from contributions, which are penalty-free.
Simplifications to know about. The headline and "average markets" figures assume your average return every single year; because real returns swing, a typical simulated market grows about 0.7 points a year slower, which is why the 85% figures matter more. All dividends are treated as qualified, IRA contributions as deductible, and one return applies to all invested accounts. Social Security benefits keep their buying power until you claim and then rise with your COLA figure; survivor benefits start at the later of 60 and the survivor's planned claiming age. The earnings test ignores the special rule in the year you reach full retirement age. Heirs are assumed to inherit brokerage accounts with a stepped-up cost basis.
Left out for now. Cost-sharing reductions and Medicaid for very low incomes, health costs rising faster than inflation, itemized deductions, after-tax (non-deductible) IRA money, home equity, and the 10-year rule for heirs beyond a flat tax rate.
